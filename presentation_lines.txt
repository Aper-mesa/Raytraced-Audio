========================================
 POSTER PRESENTATION SCRIPT
 Objective HRTF Evaluation Metrics
========================================

--- OPENING (15 sec) ---

"The spatial audio industry has been using the same evaluation metric for
decades — Log-Spectral Distortion — and it turns out it's fundamentally
broken. My thesis proves it, and proposes a fix."


--- INTRODUCTION (45 sec) ---

"HRTF — Head-Related Transfer Function — is the filter that makes headphone
audio sound like it's coming from outside your head, from a specific direction
in 3D space. It's personal to each person's anatomy.

To build or personalize HRTFs, you need a way to measure quality. The industry
standard is Log-Spectral Distortion, or LSD. The problem is that LSD only
looks at the magnitude spectrum — it is completely blind to phase. And a 2022
study by Andreopoulou and Katz showed just how badly that matters:
minimum-phase reconstruction achieves LSD below 1 dB — technically 'perfect'
— yet human listeners gave it completely different quality rankings, with a
correlation of only r ≈ 0.5."


--- METHODS (60 sec) ---

"I used the RIEC HRTF Database from Tohoku University — 20 subjects, 865
spatial positions each, 48 kHz. I applied five targeted degradation conditions,
each designed to expose a specific metric blindspot.

The most important one is minimum-phase reconstruction — it mathematically
preserves the magnitude spectrum exactly, but destroys the phase structure.

Then I computed four metrics: LSD as the baseline, ILD error which is binaural,
Group Delay error which is phase-sensitive, and ITD error. These four are
combined into a single Composite Perceptual Metric — CPM — weighted by
psychoacoustic JND thresholds. Group delay gets the highest weight of 0.35
because phase sensitivity is exactly what LSD is missing."


--- RESULTS (60 sec) ---
[Point to the results table while speaking]

"The central finding is on this table. Minimum-phase reconstruction achieves
an LSD of just 0.030 dB — the LOWEST of all five conditions. By LSD alone,
it looks like the best processing. But its CPM is 14.094 — the HIGHEST, over
five times worse than the next worst condition.

The reason is here — the Group Delay error of 2.335 ms, which is 11 to 39
times larger than any other condition.

[Point to the scatter plot]
This scatter plot shows it clearly: minimum-phase sits near zero on the LSD
axis but at the top of the CPM axis — way off the diagonal. That is the LSD
blindspot, quantified.

For all the non-phase-altering conditions, CPM behaves exactly as expected —
it increases monotonically with degradation severity. So CPM doesn't sacrifice
magnitude sensitivity to gain phase sensitivity. It captures both."


--- CONCLUSIONS (30 sec) ---

"Three contributions: First, I empirically confirmed the LSD blindspot on an
independent database. Second, Group Delay error is the key discriminator for
phase distortion. Third, CPM successfully captures both magnitude and phase
degradations in a single score.

The main limitation is that CPM hasn't been validated against subjective
listening tests yet — the weights are JND-based heuristics. That's the primary
next step: a MUSHRA listening test to verify that CPM actually correlates with
human perception."


========================================
 Q&A QUICK ANSWERS
========================================

Q: Why is LSD still being used?
A: Computationally simple and mathematically convex — ideal for optimization
   cost functions. But its perceptual validity has always been questionable.

Q: How were the CPM weights determined?
A: Set heuristically based on psychoacoustic JND thresholds. Future work will
   optimize them via regression against MUSHRA subjective scores.

Q: Why is LSD so low for minimum-phase?
A: Minimum-phase reconstruction mathematically preserves the magnitude spectrum
   exactly by construction. LSD only measures magnitude, so it reads near zero.

Q: What is the practical implication?
A: Deep learning HRTF generation models currently use LSD/MSE as loss functions.
   Replacing them with CPM would prevent training "spectrally clean but
   perceptually poor" HRTFs.

Q: Why no subjective listening test?
A: Subjective tests are expensive and time-consuming. This work first establishes
   the objective framework; MUSHRA validation is the primary future work.

Q: Why did you choose Group Delay as your phase-sensitive metric rather than
   something like phase spectrum difference directly?
A: Phase spectrum difference is highly sensitive to the reference point and
   wrapping ambiguity, making it numerically unstable across spatial positions.
   Group delay — the negative derivative of phase with respect to frequency —
   is more interpretable (it's in milliseconds), numerically stable via the
   conjugate-division formulation, and directly relates to the temporal
   fine-structure cues that the auditory system uses for localization.

Q: Could CPM produce false alarms — flagging a perceptually acceptable
   HRTF as degraded?
A: Potentially, yes — the weights are heuristic, not empirically optimized.
   For example, if a processing method introduces a large group delay shift
   that turns out to be perceptually transparent, CPM would over-penalize it.
   This is exactly why MUSHRA validation and weight optimization are the
   critical next steps.

Q: Why is the ITD error for minimum-phase so high (0.579 ms) even though
   you re-inserted the original ITD?
A: The re-insertion used onset detection — finding the first sample exceeding
   1% of peak amplitude. For HRIRs with a gradual onset, this threshold
   misidentifies the true onset, introducing a residual ITD error. A more
   robust method such as the centroid of the squared envelope would likely
   reduce this artifact.

Q: How does CPM compare to more recent metrics like PESD or BINAQUAL?
A: PESD (Armstrong et al., 2024) improves on LSD by adding threshold
   discrimination and binaural weighting, but still focuses heavily on the
   magnitude domain and may miss phase-domain distortions. BINAQUAL
   (Panah et al., 2025) explicitly models phase via phaseograms and has been
   validated against MUSHRA scores. CPM is simpler and interpretable — each
   component has a clear psychoacoustic meaning — which makes it easier to
   diagnose which dimension of quality is degraded.

Q: Why only 20 out of 51 available subjects?
A: A balanced gender subset of 10 male and 10 female subjects was selected to
   avoid confounding results with gender-specific anatomical differences, such
   as smaller pinnae in female subjects shifting pinna notch frequencies upward.
   20 subjects × 865 positions gives 17,300 spatial measurements per condition,
   which provides sufficient statistical power for cross-subject averaging.

Q: Could CPM be used as a loss function when training neural networks for
   HRTF personalization?
A: Yes, and that is one of the proposed future directions. All four CPM
   components are differentiable, so CPM can in principle be backpropagated
   through. Training with CPM instead of MSE or LSD would incentivize the
   network to preserve phase structure and binaural cues, not just match the
   magnitude spectrum.

Q: What does the 4–12 kHz pinna notch region have to do with this?
A: The pinnae create sharp spectral notches in the 4–12 kHz range that the
   brain uses to resolve elevation and front-back ambiguity. LSD averages error
   across all frequencies equally, so a catastrophic distortion of a single
   pinna notch gets diluted by "good" performance elsewhere. A future extension
   of CPM would apply frequency-dependent weighting to give this region
   proportionally greater influence.

Q: Is the RIEC database representative enough to generalize your findings?
A: It is one of the largest publicly available databases — 51 subjects, 865
   spatial positions, high temporal resolution — which is why it was chosen.
   However, generalizability across databases is not guaranteed. The SADIE II
   cross-validation proposed in future work directly addresses this concern.

Q: What would it mean practically if CPM were adopted as the industry standard?
A: HRTF personalization pipelines, compression algorithms, and deep learning
   models that currently optimize for LSD would need to be re-evaluated.
   Some techniques that are currently considered state-of-the-art by LSD
   standards — particularly minimum-phase approximations — might be rejected.
   Conversely, some techniques previously dismissed as "high distortion" by LSD
   might actually perform well perceptually if they preserve phase and binaural
   cues. It would effectively reset how the field benchmarks quality.
